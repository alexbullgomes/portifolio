Why God Makes You Wait While Everyone Else Moves On

By Alex Rodrigues Gomes · August 17, 2026 · 9 min read · Watch: 6:38

Living Devotional · 6:38 Watch on YouTube →

The short answer

Joseph received a dream from God at seventeen and did not see it fulfilled until he was thirty. In between he was sold by his brothers, enslaved in Potiphar's house, imprisoned for a crime he refused to commit, and then forgotten for two more years by the one man who promised to speak for him. The waiting was not God losing track of him. It was the thirteen years it took to build a man who could hold what the dream would eventually require.

Why I made this one

I have spent ten years making other people look good on camera. That is the job, and I love the job. But there is a particular kind of quiet that comes with it: you are the one in the back of the room with the tripod while somebody else walks into the light. You build the thing. Somebody else stands on it.

I have been in a long season of that. Finishing a theology degree, moving states, sending applications into what feels like a very well-designed void, watching people I have genuinely helped step into rooms I am still waiting to be invited into. I am not owed anything. That is not the point. The point is that I have caught myself doing the 11:47 p.m. scroll, and I have felt that specific, ugly little sting of watching someone post the thing you have been praying for.

So when I went looking for the first story to make for this channel, I did not go looking for a triumphant one. I went looking for the one that sits in the waiting the longest without flinching. That is Joseph. And the part of his story nobody puts on a coffee mug is the part that finally made sense of mine.

The dream that cost Joseph everything

Joseph was seventeen when God showed him his future, and the vision made his life dramatically worse. That is worth sitting with before anything else: in this story, the promise is not the beginning of the good part. It is the beginning of the descent.

He was the favourite, and everyone knew it. His father Jacob gave him a richly ornamented coat, the kind of garment you do not work in, and his brothers read it exactly the way it was meant to be read. Then came the dreams. Sheaves of wheat in a field, bowing to his sheaf. The sun, the moon and eleven stars, bowing to him. God was not being subtle.

And Joseph, being seventeen, told them. All of them. Out loud.

Envy fed a little every day does not stay envy. It matures into something you would not recognise at the start.

One afternoon, in a field far enough from home that nobody would hear it, they grabbed him. They stripped off the coat — the coat was always the real target — threw him into a dry cistern, and then sat down to eat their lunch while he screamed from the bottom of it. Read that again. They ate. Then they pulled him out and sold him to a passing caravan for twenty pieces of silver.

The boy with the God-given dream left Canaan in ropes, walking toward Egypt, further from the promise with every step.

He did the right thing and lost anyway

Here is where most of us quietly expect the story to correct itself, and it does not. Joseph does everything right in Egypt, and doing everything right is precisely what costs him.

He was sold into the house of Potiphar, captain of Pharaoh's guard. He could have quit on the inside — stayed technically present, functionally gone. Plenty of people do, and nobody would have blamed a trafficked seventeen-year-old for it. Instead he worked. And Scripture keeps repeating one line through this whole section like a heartbeat under the floorboards: the Lord was with Joseph. He rose until Potiphar handed him everything he owned and stopped checking.

Then Potiphar's wife noticed him. Not once — day after day, the text is careful about that. And day after day he refused. Until the afternoon she grabbed his cloak, and he did the only clean thing available to him, which was to run and leave the cloak in her hand. She kept it. She used it. She lied.

He was thrown into the king's prison for the integrity he had just demonstrated.

Faithful, and still falling. If you have ever done the right and expensive thing and watched it cost you instead of vindicate you, this is your chapter.

And even there, the same heartbeat: the Lord was with Joseph. Within a stretch of time the text does not bother to measure, he was effectively running the cell block — a leader nobody outside those walls could see, managing men in a place with no promotion to offer him.

The cruelest sentence in Joseph's story

One morning Joseph noticed that two new royal prisoners looked troubled, and he asked them why. I want to slow down on that, because it is easy to skim past. A man at the bottom of the pit, wrongly convicted, forgotten by his family for years — and he is still paying attention to somebody else's face.

Pharaoh's cupbearer had dreamed of a vine with three branches. The baker had dreamed of three baskets. God gave Joseph both interpretations, and both were exact: freedom for one man in three days, execution for the other. Both landed precisely as he said.

And Joseph asked for one thing. One. He said to the cupbearer, in effect: when it goes well for you, remember me. Mention me to Pharaoh. Get me out of this house.

Three days later the prison door opened. The cupbearer walked out into the sunlight and back into the palace, and the door swung shut again with Joseph still behind it.

Then Genesis 40 ends with what I think is the cruelest sentence in the whole story: the chief cupbearer did not remember Joseph; he forgot him.

Not “he tried and failed.” Not “the timing was wrong.” He forgot. Joseph simply stopped occupying space in the mind of the only man who could have freed him.

Two full years followed. Seven hundred and thirty mornings of waking up in that cell wondering if today was the day somebody finally remembered his name. Nobody did. Everyone he had helped had moved on, and heaven said nothing at all.

Why did God make Joseph wait thirteen years?

The honest answer the text gives is this: the waiting was formation, not delay. Joseph was seventeen when the dream came and thirty when it landed. What happened in those thirteen years was not God losing the file. It was the slow construction of a man who could survive being handed a nation.

Think about who walked into that pit. A seventeen-year-old who could not hold a revelation without announcing it to the people it would cost him the most to tell. Now think about who eventually walked out.

Because in a single night, everything moved. Pharaoh dreamed of seven fat cows devoured by seven starving ones, and not one wise man in Egypt could explain it. And in that throne room the cupbearer went pale and said the sentence he had spent two years not saying: today I remember my failure. There was a young Hebrew in the prison, and everything he interpreted came true.

They ran to get him. Joseph shaved, changed his clothes, and walked out of the pit straight into the presence of the most powerful man alive. Pharaoh said, I hear you can interpret dreams. And Joseph — thirteen years into being wronged, enslaved, lied about and forgotten — answered: it is not in me. God will give Pharaoh the answer.

That is the whole thing right there. Thirteen years of silence had not made him bitter. They had made him usable. A seventeen-year-old with that gift and no formation would have taken the credit in the throne room, and Egypt would have starved under a man who thought the power was his.

Same day: the signet ring, the fine linen, the gold chain, and the second chariot of Egypt. From the pit to the palace in one morning — and thirteen years of preparation to make that morning survivable.

Here is what Joseph could not possibly see from inside the cell, and what you cannot see from inside yours: the forgetfulness of men is not the forgetfulness of God. In that silence, while absolutely nothing appeared to be happening, a governor was being built.

Three things to do while you are still waiting

I do not think the point of this story is to sit still and feel spiritual about it. Joseph did specific things in that cell, and they are repeatable. Here is what I have actually been trying to do with them.

  1. Do the invisible work with excellence. Joseph ran Potiphar's house and then ran a prison block, and neither came with an audience or a promotion. He did them properly anyway. Whatever is in front of you right now that nobody is watching — the unglamorous file, the small client, the thing with no title attached — do that one at full strength. Faithfulness in the dark is never wasted material. It is the only place certain skills can be built.
  2. Look up from the feed and serve the person in front of you. The single most striking detail in Genesis 40 is that a forgotten prisoner noticed two troubled faces and asked what was wrong. Comparison is a paralysis, and the reliable way out of it is not to think harder about yourself — it is to help somebody. Turn the phone face down and go be useful to one actual person this week.
  3. Change the question you are asking. Stop asking why not yet. It has no answer you can act on and it will hollow you out. Start asking: what is this season building in me that my calling will one day require? That question has an answer, usually an uncomfortable one, and it converts waiting from dead time into formation.

The dream God gave Joseph at seventeen came true at thirty, larger than anything he had pictured in that field. And the two years he spent being completely forgotten by a man in a palace turned out to be two of the most load-bearing years of his life.

Being forgotten by people never once meant being forgotten by God. It did not for him. It does not for you.

Questions people ask about Joseph's waiting

How long was Joseph in prison?

Genesis does not give an exact figure for the prison term alone, but it does anchor the total. Joseph was seventeen when his brothers sold him (Genesis 37:2) and thirty when he entered Pharaoh's service (Genesis 41:46) — thirteen years across slavery and imprisonment combined. Genesis 41:1 tells us that two full years of that passed after he interpreted the cupbearer's dream and was forgotten.

What does Genesis 40:23 mean?

Genesis 40:23 reads, "Yet the chief cupbearer did not remember Joseph; he forgot him." It is a deliberate double statement — the text says it twice for emphasis. The cupbearer did not weigh Joseph's request and decide against it; Joseph simply left his mind. It marks the beginning of the two years of silence in Genesis 41:1, and it is the low point of the narrative: the one human being positioned to free Joseph stops thinking about him entirely.

How old was Joseph when he became governor of Egypt?

Thirty. Genesis 41:46 states plainly that Joseph was thirty years old when he entered the service of Pharaoh. Since he was seventeen when he was sold into Egypt, the gap between the dream and its fulfilment was thirteen years.

Does God forget people who are waiting?

The Joseph narrative answers this by contrast rather than argument. People forget — his brothers, and then the cupbearer, explicitly. But through the worst stretches of the story the text repeats one refrain: "the Lord was with Joseph" (Genesis 39:2, 39:21). The waiting was real and the silence was real, and neither indicated absence. What looked like thirteen wasted years was the preparation that made the position survivable.
